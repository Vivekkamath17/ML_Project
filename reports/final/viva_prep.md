### Question 1: Why did your team prioritize Precision-Recall AUC (PR-AUC) over Receiver Operating Characteristic AUC (ROC-AUC) as the primary evaluation metric?

**Evidence-Based Response:**
In our rolling 3-week attrition prediction task ($k=3$), the validation cohort exhibits severe class imbalance: the empirical positive event rate is only **$2.37\%$** (3,901 positive dropout events across 164,799 weekly rows). 

Under severe class imbalance, ROC-AUC is misleadingly optimistic because its horizontal axis is False Positive Rate:
$$\text{FPR} = \frac{\text{FP}}{\text{FP} + \text{TN}}$$
Because the pool of true negatives ($\text{TN} = 160,898$) is immense, a model can generate thousands of false alarms ($\text{FP}$) with only a negligible increase in FPR. Consequently, a model with a seemingly strong ROC-AUC of $0.78$ can produce an operational alert precision below $8\%$.

In contrast, Precision-Recall curves plot:
$$\text{Precision} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FP}} \quad \text{against} \quad \text{Recall} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FN}}$$
PR-AUC isolates the positive class without incorporating $\text{TN}$. The uninformative baseline for ROC-AUC is always $0.50$, whereas for PR-AUC it is the empirical base rate ($0.0237$). Our champion model's PR-AUC of $0.0786$ represents a **$3.32\times$ lift over random chance**, directly reflecting how many advisor outreach calls will reach students truly in academic jeopardy.

### Question 2: Why was it essential to cluster bootstrap resamples by `id_student` rather than resampling individual weekly observation rows?

**Evidence-Based Response:**
Our dataset contains 164,799 weekly observations generated by 6,474 unique student registrations. Individual students contribute up to 39 weekly observations across the presentation. These observations violate the standard independent-and-identically-distributed (i.i.d.) assumption due to strong intra-subject correlation: a student who is disengaged in Week 4 is highly likely to remain disengaged in Week 5.

If we performed standard row-wise bootstrap resampling:
1. Observations from the same student would appear simultaneously in both the bootstrap resample and out-of-bag evaluations, causing temporal autocorrelation leakage.
2. Standard errors would be artificially deflated by a factor of roughly $\sqrt{N_{\text{rows}} / N_{\text{students}}} \approx \sqrt{25.4} \approx 5.0$, producing unrealistically narrow confidence intervals.

By implementing **learner-clustered bootstrap resampling (500 resamples clustered by `id_student`)**, we sample students with replacement and pull all of their weekly records together. This rigorously preserves student-level variance and yields honest, reproducible confidence intervals.

### Question 3: Why is presentation `2014J` strictly sealed, and why was `allow_test=True` prohibited during your work?

**Evidence-Based Response:**
Presentation `2014J` serves as our sealed, out-of-year temporal test split. In machine learning research, repeated evaluation on a designated test split during exploratory analysis, threshold tuning, or model selection causes subtle, cumulative data leakage—a phenomenon known as "test set snooping" or adaptive overfitting.

To prevent this:
1. Our data loader (`src/models/data.py`) contains a hard guard that raises a `ValueError` if `split == "test"` unless `allow_test=True` is explicitly passed.
2. All baseline comparisons, model zoo benchmarks, calibration fits, algorithmic fairness audits, and Streamlit dashboard components were developed exclusively on the validation split (`2014B`, $N=6,474$ enrolled learners, weeks $\ge 2$).
3. Presentation `2014J` will be unsealed exactly once by our designated teammate to assess genuine out-of-year transferability. Our code is verified by unit tests (`test_sealed_test_isolation_in_app_code`) to ensure zero access to `2014J`.

### Question 4: Under your 5-seed benchmark, all four tree models tied statistically. Why did CatBoost, XGBoost, and Random Forest fail to beat LightGBM v3?

**Evidence-Based Response:**
Under our 5-seed protocol with 500-iteration learner-clustered paired bootstrap testing, the paired differences in validation PR-AUC relative to LightGBM v3 were:
- **CatBoost**: $\Delta\text{PR} = -0.0000$ [95% CI: $-0.0027, +0.0022$] — **Statistically Tied**
- **XGBoost**: $\Delta\text{PR} = -0.0005$ [95% CI: $-0.0020, +0.0011$] — **Statistically Tied**
- **Random Forest**: $\Delta\text{PR} = -0.0008$ [95% CI: $-0.0039, +0.0022$] — **Statistically Tied**

Every single 95% confidence interval for $\Delta\text{PR}$ and $\Delta\text{ROC}$ spans zero. This empirical tie occurs because performance on tabular clickstream data is **feature-constrained rather than algorithm-constrained**. 

Our 38 sanctioned features capture student trajectory via summary aggregates (e.g. `days_since_last_activity`, `weighted_score_to_date`, `weekly_clicks`). Whether decision trees are constructed leaf-wise (LightGBM), depth-wise (XGBoost), symmetrically (CatBoost), or as bagged forests, they identify the same primary decision boundaries (e.g. inactivity $> 14$ days combined with missed first assessment). 

Consequently, model selection reduces to operational engineering criteria: LightGBM v3 was selected as champion because it trains in **3.3 seconds**, requires only **0.43 MB** disk footprint, and executes CPU inference in **13.5 ms per 1,000 rows**.

### Question 5: In your fairness audit, female students had a lower True Positive Rate ($29.4\%$) than male students ($39.2\%$). Does this prove the model is discriminatory against women?

**Evidence-Based Response:**
No. This disparity is a direct mathematical consequence of **Kleinberg's Impossibility Theorem** (Kleinberg et al., 2016).

In our validation cohort (`2014B`), male and female students exhibit substantially different baseline withdrawal rates:
- **Male base rate**: $2.66\%$ (2,483 positive dropout weeks across 93,247 male learner-weeks).
- **Female base rate**: $1.98\%$ (1,418 positive dropout weeks across 71,552 female learner-weeks).

Crucially, the model's discriminative capability between genders is **statistically identical**:
- **Male ROC-AUC**: $0.774$ [95% CI: $0.758, 0.789$]
- **Female ROC-AUC**: $0.773$ [95% CI: $0.754, 0.792$]
- **ROC Gap**: **$0.0011$** (effectively zero).

Kleinberg's theorem proves mathematically that if two groups have unequal base rates ($p_1 \neq p_2$), a classifier cannot simultaneously satisfy:
1. Equal True Positive Rates ($\text{TPR}_1 = \text{TPR}_2$, Equal Opportunity)
2. Equal False Positive Rates ($\text{FPR}_1 = \text{FPR}_2$)
3. Equal Positive Predictive Value ($\text{PPV}_1 = \text{PPV}_2$, Predictive Parity)
unless the classifier achieves perfect $100\%$ accuracy. 

When a global decision threshold (such as our Top-10% weekly caseload cut) is applied across a population where women have lower base attrition, female risk scores naturally fall lower in the ordinal distribution, resulting in a lower selection rate ($7.7\%$ vs $11.8\%$) and consequently a lower observed recall ($29.4\%$ vs $39.2\%$). The disparity is governed by cohort base-rate differences, not algorithmic prejudice.

### Question 6: Why is the learner-level lift only $1.4\times$ to $1.9\times$ over random selection under a 10% capacity policy?

**Evidence-Based Response:**
When advisors evaluate retention systems, they care about unique human students caught over a semester, not isolated weekly rows. Under our default Top-10% weekly capacity policy:
- Unique learners enrolled: **6,474**
- Unique eventual dropouts: **1,395** (learner base rate $= 21.55\%$)
- Unique learners flagged at least once: **2,269** ($35.05\%$ of enrolled cohort)
- Eventual dropouts caught at least once: **777** ($55.70\%$ student-level recall)
- Student-level alert precision: $777 / 2,269 = 34.24\%$

The learner-level lift is calculated as:
$$\text{Lift} = \frac{\text{Learner Precision}}{\text{Cohort Base Rate}} = \frac{34.24\%}{21.55\%} = \mathbf{1.59\times}$$
Across different tree models, this lift ranges between $1.4\times$ and $1.9\times$.

The lift is modest because of **quiet, non-behavioral attrition**. In distance education, over $40\%$ of students who withdraw do not experience a multi-week trajectory of declining clicks. Instead, they disengage abruptly due to external life shocks (job relocation, illness, family bereavement, financial crisis). Because VLE servers only log digital clicks and cannot monitor external life events, these students appear completely normal in the logs until the day they unregister.

### Question 7: How did you ensure that demographic attributes were strictly quarantined and never influenced model training?

**Evidence-Based Response:**
We enforced a multi-layered architectural quarantine:
1. **Repository Feature Manifest**: `config/features.yaml` strictly declares the sanctioned 38 continuous and 3 boolean features. Zero demographic fields exist in this manifest.
2. **Physical Data Isolation**: Demographic fields (`gender`, `region`, `highest_education`, `imd_band`, `age_band`, `disability`) were extracted into an isolated file (`data/processed/audit_demographics.parquet`).
3. **Late-Binding Join**: Demographics are joined to model outputs solely at evaluation time via `(code_module, code_presentation, id_student)` within `src/fairness/audit.py`.
4. **Automated Grep Verification Test**: We wrote an automated unit test (`test_demographic_isolation_grep_rule` in `tests/test_fairness.py`) that recursively searches all Python source code in `src/models/`, `src/data/`, and `config/`. The test asserts that strings like `gender`, `imd_band`, and `highest_education` appear nowhere in model training or feature transformation routines. The test passes with 100% compliance.

### Question 9: What does your calibration curve reveal about uncalibrated tree models, and how was it corrected?

**Evidence-Based Response:**
Evaluating calibration curves (reliability diagrams) on validation predictions revealed two distinct distortions:
1. **Mild Global Under-Prediction**: Raw models predict an average probability of $\sim 1.9\%$, slightly under-predicting the validation base rate ($2.37\%$) because training presentations had a lower positive prevalence ($1.85\%$).
2. **Severe High-Risk Over-Confidence**: In high predicted risk bins ($\hat{p} > 0.20$), raw uncalibrated models are severely over-confident. When the model outputs probabilities between $25\%$ and $35\%$, the true observed attrition rate is only $10\%$ to $12\%$.

This occurs because tree algorithms optimize split purity (entropy or Gini), driving leaf probabilities toward extreme values. 

We rectified this by applying post-hoc **Isotonic Regression** and **Platt Scaling** calibrated across validation folds. Isotonic calibration flattened the over-confidence distortion, reducing Expected Calibration Error (ECE) from $0.00497$ to **$0.00084$** (an **$83\%$ error reduction**) and bringing Calibration-in-the-Large (CITL) to $-0.00001$, ensuring that predicted probabilities accurately match observed empirical rates.

### Question 10: In your mitigation experiment, group-specific thresholds reduced Equalized Odds differences by 34%. Why not deploy group-specific thresholds in production?

**Evidence-Based Response:**
While our in-sample experiment on a 50/50 validation split demonstrated that tuning decision thresholds per demographic group reduced Equalized Odds difference from $0.461$ to $0.304$ while maintaining recall ($36.8\%$), deploying group-specific thresholds in a live educational institution is fraught with severe ethical, legal, and operational hazards:

1. **Disparate Treatment Concerns**: Operating different probability thresholds based on protected demographic attributes (e.g. requiring a lower risk threshold to flag one group than another) constitutes explicit disparate treatment under UK and EU non-discrimination law.
2. **In-Sample Overfitting**: Demographic thresholds tuned on validation cohort `2014B` reflect presentation-specific enrollment distributions. When applied to future presentations with shifted demographic proportions, fixed group thresholds can actually exacerbate disparities.
3. **Paternalistic Deficit Framing**: Setting lower thresholds for disadvantaged cohorts increases their false positive rate, potentially subjecting them to disproportionate surveillance and deficit-framed outreach.

Therefore, our report explicitly characterizes mitigation as **"measured and reduced, never fixed"**, and recommends that institutions address retention via universal curriculum accessibility and proactive support rather than algorithmic threshold manipulation.

### Question 11: Why did univariate models based on click count or inactivity fail to achieve acceptable precision?

**Evidence-Based Response:**
Univariate Logistic Regression models yielded poor results:
- `weekly_clicks`: PR-AUC $= 0.0340$ ($1.44\times$ base rate)
- `days_since_last_activity`: PR-AUC $= 0.0334$ ($1.41\times$ base rate)
- `weighted_score_to_date`: PR-AUC $= 0.0320$ ($1.35\times$ base rate)

They fail because student disengagement is multidimensional:
- A student with low weekly clicks might be reading downloaded textbook PDFs or offline materials, remaining fully committed.
- A student with high clicks might be frantically clicking through orientation pages without submitting coursework.
- Inactivity duration (`days_since_last_activity`) is only informative in the context of the course calendar: 10 days of inactivity during a scheduled semester reading break is normal, whereas 10 days of inactivity immediately preceding an assessment deadline is critical.

Only multivariate models that interact activity recency with submission timeliness and pacing ratios can separate benign study pauses from genuine academic withdrawal.

### Question 12: How do your models handle missing assessment scores before students submit their first assignment?

**Evidence-Based Response:**
Missing assessment scores represent a major technical challenge because prior to Week 6, features like `mean_score_submitted`, `last_score`, and `score_slope` have no observed values.

Our models handle this via two distinct mechanisms:
1. **Tree Models (LightGBM, CatBoost, XGBoost)**: Use **native directional splitting**. During training, the algorithm evaluates whether placing missing values on the left or right branch maximizes objective gain. Missingness is treated as an informative, distinct state rather than an imputation artifact.
2. **Random Forest & Linear Models**: Linear models fail with NaNs. In Phase 3, we augmented Random Forest with **5 binary missingness indicators** (e.g. `mean_score_submitted_is_missing`, `last_score_is_missing`), imputing continuous NaNs with training medians. The missingness indicator allows the model to isolate students who have not yet submitted assignments from students who submitted and failed.

### Question 13: What is the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) in early course weeks, and why is early detection so challenging?

**Evidence-Based Response:**
In early course weeks (Weeks 1 to 5), the best observed Matthews Correlation Coefficient across all models is approximately **$\text{MCC} \approx 0.14$**.

Early detection is challenging due to the **"Assessment-Driven Signal Shift"**:
- Prior to Week 6, the only available features are static enrollment metadata and VLE clickstreams. VLE clicks have high variance: diligent students often study offline, while disengaged students may login briefly without studying.
- Strong predictive signal emerges only after Week 8, when the first formal assessment deadlines pass. Once `weighted_score_to_date` and `unsubmitted_assessments` are populated, MCC rises significantly and PR-AUC multipliers cross $3.5\times$ to $5.0\times$. 

This creates an operational trade-off: when advising intervention is most actionable (Weeks 1–4), model signal is weakest; when model signal is definitive (Week 10+), student withdrawal is often already irreversible.

### Question 14: How does a weekly capacity policy differ from a fixed probability threshold in operational deployment?

**Evidence-Based Response:**
In academic advising services, staff resources are fixed: a retention office might have 4 advisors capable of conducting 150 supportive phone calls per week.

- **Fixed Probability Threshold ($\hat{p} \ge \theta$)**: Setting an absolute probability cutoff (e.g. $\theta = 0.05$) creates extreme caseload volatility. In early quiet weeks, only 20 students might cross the threshold. In Week 8 following an assessment deadline, 800 students might trigger alerts simultaneously, completely overwhelming advisor capacity and causing hundreds of alerts to go unaddressed.
- **Weekly Capacity Policy (Top-X% Caseload)**: By ranking active students weekly and flagging the top $10\%$ most disengaged students, the advising service receives a predictable, prioritized caseload every Monday morning. Our Streamlit dashboard implements this operational reality via a live capacity slider (1% to 30%), computing exact precision, recall, and caseload counts in real time.

### Question 15: What are the primary ethical and governance risks of deploying automated Early Warning Systems in higher education?

**Evidence-Based Response:**
Deploying algorithmic retention systems introduces substantial ethical hazards that institutions must actively govern:
1. **Deficit Framing and Stigmatization**: If an alert labels a student as "high risk", advisors may unconsciously adopt lowered expectations, or students may experience stereotype threat and anxiety, transforming the prediction into a self-fulfilling prophecy.
2. **Paternalistic Outreach**: Alerts must never lead to punitive interventions (e.g. threatening administrative withdrawal). They must trigger positive, supportive inquiries (e.g. offering tutoring, extension guidance, or wellbeing check-ins).
3. **Digital Exclusion Bias**: Students with limited home internet or hardware may access resources offline, generating sparse clickstreams that falsely flag them as disengaged.
4. **Appropriate Human Authority**: Models must function strictly as **decision-support tools**. High-stakes institutional decisions—such as academic dismissal, financial aid adjustments, or final grading—must never be automated.
